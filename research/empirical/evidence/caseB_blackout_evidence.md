**Quoting conventions.** Text was extracted with pdftotext. In quotes, words hyphenated across line breaks are rejoined. Sidebar icon labels that interleave with body text in the extraction ("Cause 2 Inadequate Situational Awareness", "Recommendation N, page M") are left out of the quotes. Otherwise the wording is verbatim. Ellipses (...) mark omissions I made. Ellipses written as ". . ." are the report's own.

**Tag rule.** CONTEMPORANEOUS means the fact was known to, or available to, at least one operating organization at the time on Aug 14, 2003, or before it. RETROSPECTIVE means the investigation established it afterwards (modelling, field evidence, post-event testing). Both reports were written after the event. The tag describes when the *fact* was available, not when it was published.

**Confidence.** High: stated directly in a primary source with a time or figure. Medium: stated but hedged, or the two primary sources differ. Low: inference or indirect.

## Q2. Authority and control

### 2a. Who had authority (statements supporting "authority existed")

1. **All operators and RCs had authority to redispatch and shed load, under NERC policy.** "System operators and reliability coordinators, by NERC policy, have the responsibility and the authority to take actions up to and including emergency generation redispatch and shedding firm load to preserve system security. On August 14, because they either did not know or understand enough about system conditions at the time, system operators at FE, MISO, PJM, or AEP did not call for emergency actions." TF2004 p.62 (box "Handling Emergencies by Shedding Load and Arranging TLRs"). Authority: CONTEMPORANEOUS (it existed then). Conf. High.
2. "The dispatchers at FE, MISO and other control areas or reliability coordinators have authority--and under NERC operating policies, responsibility--to take such action, but the occasion to do so is relatively rare." TF2004 p.62. High.
3. **Control areas have primary responsibility.** "1. Control area operators have primary responsibility for reliability." TF2004 p.13. NERC Policy 5 quoted: "Each system and CONTROL AREA shall promptly take appropriate action to relieve any abnormal conditions, which jeopardize reliable Interconnection operation." TF2004 p.13. High.
4. **FE failed to use authority it had.** NERC-identified "Other Problems": "FE operations personnel did not exercise the authority to alleviate the operating security limit violation as required by NERC Policy 5, Section C, Requirement 2." "FE did not exercise a load reduction program to relieve the critical system operating conditions as required by NERC Policy 2, Section A, Requirement 1.2." TF2004 p.21. High.
5. TF2004 p.22 endnote: Policy 5 "gives system operators responsibility and authority to alleviate operating security limit violations using timely and appropriate actions." High.
6. **FE had written procedures that included shedding firm load.** "FE has specific written procedures and plans for dealing with resource deficiencies, voltage depressions, and overloads, and these include instructions to adjust generators and trip firm loads. ... But FE did not follow any of these procedures on August 14, because FE did not know for most of that time that its system might need such treatment." TF2004 p.67. High.

### 2b. Statements indicating limited or ambiguous authority (especially MISO)

1. **MISO lacked signed authority over FE.** "Although MISO is the reliability coordinator for FE, on August 14 FE was not a signatory to the MISO Transmission Owners Agreement and was not under the MISO tariff, so MISO did not have the necessary authority as FE's Reliability Coordinator as required by NERC Policy 9, Section B, Requirement 2." TF2004 p.20. CONTEMPORANEOUS (status on Aug 14). High.
2. **Yet MISO should have issued directives.** "Although lacking authority under a signed agreement, MISO as reliability coordinator nevertheless should have issued directives to FE to return system operation to a safe and reliable level as required by NERC Policy 9, Section B, Requirement 2, before the cascading outages occurred." TF2004 p.20. High. *(The report treats MISO's authority as both lacking under contract and required under NERC policy.)*
3. **Structural limit on MISO.** "The other is that MISO has less reliability-related authority over its control area members than PJM has over its members. Arguably, this lack of authority makes day-to-day reliability operations more challenging. Note, however, that (1) FERC's authority to require that MISO have greater authority over its members is limited" TF2004 p.14 (box "Institutional Complexities and Reliability in the Midwest"). High.
4. **MISO's role in practice.** "MISO was hindered because it lacked clear visibility, responsibility, authority, and ability to take the actions needed in this circumstance. MISO had interpretive and operational tools and a large amount of system data, but had a limited view of FE's system. In MISO's function as FE's reliability coordinator, its primary task was to initiate and implement TLRs, recognize and solve congestion problems in less dramatic reliability circumstances with longer solution time periods than those which existed on August 14, and provide assistance as requested." TF2004 p.67. High.
5. **No shared understanding of authorities.** "On August 14, the principal entities involved in the blackout did not have a shared understanding of whether the grid was in an emergency condition, nor did they have a common understanding of the functions, responsibilities, capabilities, and authorities of reliability coordinators and control areas under emergency or near-emergency conditions." TF2004 p.158 (Rec. 20). High.
6. NERC2004 p.98 (deficiency 4): "Reliability coordinators and control areas have adopted differing interpretations of the functions, responsibilities, authorities, and capabilities needed to operate a reliable power system. For example, MISO delegated substantial portions of its reliability oversight functions to its member control areas and did not provide a redundant set of eyes adequate for monitoring a wide-area view of reliability in its area of responsibility." High.
7. **Post-event fix implies authority had been unclear.** NERC (Feb 10, 2004) "Required that MISO reevaluate its operating agreements with member entities to verify its authority to address operating issues, including ... the deployment and redispatch of real and reactive reserves for emergency response, and the authority to direct actions during system emergencies, including shedding load." TF2004 p.153; NERC2004 p.118. RETROSPECTIVE (remedy). High.
8. **The RC relationship was recent.** "MISO became the reliability coordinator for FirstEnergy on February 1, 2003, when the ECAR-MET reliability coordinator office operated by AEP became part of PJM. FirstEnergy became a full member of MISO on October 1, 2003, six weeks after the blackout." NERC2004 p.8. High.
9. **PJM and AEP across the seam.** AEP's RC is PJM (TF2004 p.13). "PJM and MISO as reliability coordinators lacked procedures or guidelines between their respective organizations regarding the coordination of actions to address an operating security limit violation observed by one of them in the other's area due to a contingency near their common boundary" (Violation 6), TF2004 p.20. Around 15:50, AEP and PJM "still found no practical way to mitigate these overloads across utility and reliability coordinator boundaries." TF2004 p.66. High.
10. **No backup control authority for FE.** "FE did not have the ability to transfer control of its power system to an alternate center or authority during system emergencies. FE had not arranged for a backup control center or backup system control and monitoring functions." NERC2004 p.100. High.
11. **NERC itself had no enforcement authority.** "...NERC does not have the authority to enforce compliance with the standards." TF2004 p.17; also p.21. High.

### 2c. Could MISO or neighbors see FE's problems?

- **Data was flowing (for "visible").** "The EMS also continued to send its normal and expected collection of information on to other monitoring points and authorities, including MISO and AEP. Thus these entities continued to receive accurate information about the status and condition of FE's power system after the time when FE's EMS alarms failed." TF2004 p.53. "The system was automatically transferring accurate real-time information about FE's system conditions to computers at AEP, MISO, and PJM." TF2004 p.67. High.
- **MISO missed breaker alarms (for "not seen").** "MISO received indications of breaker trips in FE that registered in MISO's alarms; however, the alarms were missed. These alarms require a look-up to link the flagged breaker with the associated line or equipment ... MISO operators did not have the capability to click on the on-screen alarm indicator to display the underlying information." TF2004 p.55 ("Who Saw What?"). High.
- "MISO did not discover that Harding-Chamberlin had tripped until after the blackout, when MISO reviewed the breaker operation log that evening." TF2004 p.60. High.
- "Harding-Chamberlin was not one of the flowgates that MISO monitored ... Because their EMS system topology processor had not yet been linked to recognize line failures, it did not connect the breaker information to the loss of a transmission line." TF2004 p.60. High.
- **MISO RTCA.** "The contingency analysis showed several hundred violations around 15:00 EDT. This included some FE violations, which MISO ... operators discussed with PJM ... There is no indication that MISO addressed this issue. It is not known whether MISO identified the developing Sammis-Star problem." TF2004 p.55. Medium: this sits awkwardly with the statement that SE/RTCA were "effectively out of service between 12:15 EDT and 16:04 EDT" (p.49).
- **Flowgate tool.** It "prompted MISO to call FE to discuss the Hanna-Juniper line problem. It would not have identified problems south of Star since that was not part of the flowgate". TF2004 p.55. NERC2004 p.97: with correct topology, the FMT "would have identified a contingency overload on the Star-Juniper 345-kV line for the loss of the Hanna-Juniper 345-kV line as early as 15:10." High.
- **AEP saw its own end.** "AEP had contingency analysis that covered lines into Star." AEP "called FE three times between 14:35 EDT and 15:45 EDT". TF2004 p.55. "PJM and AEP recognized the overload on Star-South Canton, but had not expected it because their earlier contingency analysis did not examine enough lines within the FE system". TF2004 p.62. High.
- **Critical-facility definitions.** "MISO's procedure in effect on August 14 was to request FE to identify critical facilities on its system to MISO." NERC2004 p.100. High.

### 2d. Was any organization restricted from acting?

- No passage says any entity was *prohibited* from acting. The documented constraints are: (i) MISO's lack of contractual authority under the TO agreement/tariff (TF2004 p.20), together with the finding that it should still have issued directives; (ii) the lack of PJM-MISO seam procedures (p.20); (iii) the slow and unsuitable TLR tool (p.62); (iv) FE's limited quick load-shed means (p.66, see Q3). Overall the reports put inaction down to lack of awareness ("because they either did not know or understand enough", p.62), not to prohibition.

## Q4. Dependencies and restoration

### 4a. Organizational / informational dependencies (documented)

1. **FE operators on EMS alarms.** "Power system operators rely heavily on audible and on-screen alarms, plus alarm logs, to reveal any significant changes in their system's conditions." TF2004 p.52. "FE operators relied heavily on the alarm processor for situational awareness, since they did not have any other large-scale visualization tool such as a dynamic map board." NERC2004 p.32. High.
2. **No alarm-on-alarm-failure.** "...it can also be set up to alarm them if the alarm system itself fails to perform properly. FE's EMS did not have such a notification system." TF2004 pp.52-53. High.
3. **FE IT to FE operators (communication link failed).** "When the back-up server failed, it too sent an auto-page to FE's IT staff. They did not notify control room operators of the problem." TF2004 p.54. NERC Cause 1b: "nor did they have a formal procedure to do so." NERC2004 p.95. High.
4. **Failover propagated the fault (a dependent redundancy).** "because the alarm application moved intact onto the backup while still stalled and ineffective, the backup server failed 13 minutes later, at 14:54 EDT." TF2004 p.54. High.
5. **Chain from SCADA to SE to contingency analysis.** "Operators look at potential problems that could arise on their systems by using contingency analyses, driven from state estimation, that are fed by data collected by the SCADA system." TF2004 p.47. "MISO real-time contingency analysis, which operates only if the state estimator solves, did not operate properly in automatic mode again until after the blackout." NERC2004 p.97. High. *(Documented as strictly sequential: RTCA needs a solved SE.)*
6. **MISO SE on outside line-status data.** "Line status information within MISO's reliability coordination area is transmitted to MISO by the ECAR data network or direct links and is intended to be automatically linked to the SE. This requires coordinated data naming as well as instructions that link the data to the tools. For this line, the automatic linkage of line status to the state estimator had not yet been established." TF2004 p.48. The Stuart-Atlanta status "had not been automatically linked to MISO's state estimator." p.49. High.
7. **MISO on the SDX (not real-time).** "MISO was using its Flowgate Monitoring Tool (FMT) ... However, the FMT was receiving information on facility outages from the NERC SDX, which is not intended as a real-time information system and is not required to be updated in real-time." NERC2004 p.97. "There is no current requirement for how quickly asset owners must report changes in element status (such as a line outage) to the SDX". TF2004 p.51. DPL did not post the Stuart-Atlanta outage to the SDX (TF2004 p.71, endnote 8). High.
8. **MISO on PJM by voice** for Stuart-Atlanta status (15:29-15:33). TF2004 p.49, p.57. High.
9. **FE contingency analysis run manually because of a long-standing fault.** "FE indicated that it has experienced problems with the automatic contingency analysis operation since the system was installed in 1995. As a result, FE operators or engineers ran contingency analysis manually rather than automatically ... the contingency analysis model was likely running but not consulted at any point in the afternoon of August 14." TF2004 p.71, endnote 7. High.
10. **Inside the FE control room.** Clue-sharing failed because of "1. Physical separation of operators ... 2. The lack of a shared electronic log ... 3. Lack of systematic procedures to brief incoming staff at shift change times. 4. Infrequent training". TF2004 p.67. High.
11. **AEP/PJM to MISO/FE across the seam.** No joint procedure (Violation 6, TF2004 p.20). PJM and MISO Sammis-Star readings did not match (p.66). Line ratings differed: "FE was operating Sammis-Star assuming that the 345-kV line was rated for summer normal use at 1,310 MVA, with a summer emergency limit rating of 1,310 MVA. In contrast, MISO, PJM and AEP were using a more conservative rating of 950 MVA normal and 1,076 MVA emergency". TF2004 p.72, endnote 44. High.
12. **FE dependence on imports** to Cleveland-Akron: "At 15:00 EDT on August 14, FE's load was approximately 12,080 MW, and they were importing about 2,575 MW, 21% of their total." TF2004 p.49. High.

### 4b. Physical dependencies (line loading sequence)

- "As each line failed, its outage increased the loading on the remaining lines". TF2004 p.57. "Incremental line current and temperature increases, escalated by the loss of Harding-Chamberlin, caused more sag on the Hanna-Juniper line". p.57. High.
- Ratings at trip: Harding-Chamberlin 44%; Hanna-Juniper 88%; Star-South Canton 93% of emergency (pp.57-63). After Hanna-Juniper, "over 1,200 MVA of power flow had to find a new path" (p.61). "Star-South Canton's loading jumped from an estimated 82% of normal to 120% of normal ... and remained at the 120% level for 10 minutes before tripping out." (p.64). High.
- After Star-South Canton: "flows increased greatly on the 138-kV system toward Cleveland ... At the same time, power flows increased on the Sammis-Star 345-kV line due to the 138-kV line trips--the only remaining paths into Cleveland from the south." p.64. Sammis-Star "above 120% of rating" before tripping (p.69). High.
- Common-mode cause: "These three lines experienced non-random, common mode failures due to unchecked tree growth." NERC2004 p.96. High.
- Eastlake 5 dependence: "when Eastlake 5 was modeled as in service and fully available in those simulations, all overloads above emergency limits were eliminated, even with the loss of Harding-Chamberlin." TF2004 p.49. RETROSPECTIVE. High.
- Perry: "had Perry been lost after the Harding-Chamberlin line went down at 15:05 EDT, the Cleveland-Akron area would have been close to voltage collapse." TF2004 p.41. RETROSPECTIVE. High.
- Pre-15:05 security: "None of these contingencies resulted in a violation ... prior to the trip of FE's Harding-Chamberlin 345-kV line." TF2004 p.41. RETROSPECTIVE. High.

### 4c. Sequential vs parallel

The reports **do not explicitly classify** steps as sequential or parallel. What is documented:
- **Sequential (explicit):** SCADA, then SE, then RTCA (Q4a-5). Identify emergency, then determine action: "To handle an emergency effectively a dispatcher must first identify the emergency situation and then determine effective action." TF2004 p.66. TLR levels: emergency redispatch/load-shed first, then TLR 3/5 "After a secure state is reached" (p.62). IT detection, then IT to operator notification, then operator alternate monitoring (NERC Causes 1a-1c, pp.95-96: "there still would have been a minimum of 40 minutes, from 14:14 to 14:54, during which the support staff was unaware").
- **Sequential (physical):** each line trip increased loading on the next (Figure 5.12, "Cumulative Effects of Sequential Outages", p.64).
- **Concurrent (documented as happening at the same time, not as designed parallelism):** MISO's SE troubleshooting (12:15-16:04) ran alongside FE's EMS failures (14:14-15:59). AEP/PJM TLR work ran "all while AEP and PJM were discussing TLR relief options" as Star-South Canton tripped (p.57). FE IT reboots (15:46-15:59) overlapped with operator response.
- *Analyst note (inference, Low confidence):* nothing in either report describes load shedding by FE and directives by MISO as requiring a strict order. The post-event requirement lets either "MISO or the FE system operator" trigger the 1,500-MW reduction (p.151), which implies parallel trigger paths after the event, not on Aug 14.

## Q6. Root causes as stated

### TF2004 (p.18, box "Causes of the Blackout's Initiation"; violations pp.19-22)

- "There are four groups of causes for the blackout:"
- **Group 1:** "FirstEnergy and ECAR failed to assess and understand the inadequacies of FE's system, particularly with respect to voltage instability and the vulnerability of the Cleveland-Akron area, and FE did not operate its system with appropriate voltage criteria." (Note: "This cause was not identified in the Task Force's Interim Report.") Icon label: "Inadequate System Understanding" (Cause 1).
- **Group 2:** "Inadequate situational awareness at FirstEnergy. FE did not recognize or understand the deteriorating condition of its system." Sub-items A-E (contingency analysis, awareness of tool state, IT-operations communication, post-repair testing, backup monitoring tools). Cause 2.
- **Group 3:** "FE failed to manage adequately tree growth in its transmission rights-of-way. This failure was the common cause of the outage of three FE 345-kV transmission lines and one 138-kV line." Cause 3 ("Inadequate Tree Trimming").
- **Group 4:** "Failure of the interconnected grid's reliability organizations to provide effective real-time diagnostic support." Sub-items A-D (Stuart-Atlanta not in SE; "non-real-time data to support real-time 'flowgate' monitoring"; no way to identify breaker operations; no PJM-MISO joint procedures). Cause 4 ("Inadequate RC Diagnostic Support").
- An extra category: "Failures to act by FirstEnergy or others to solve the growing problem, due to the other causes." (p.20)
- Framing: "they reflect long-standing institutional failures and weaknesses" (p.17). "Clear standards with mandatory compliance ... might have averted the start of this blackout." (p.17)
- **Seven NERC violations** (p.22): 1 FE did not return to a safe state within 30 min after Chamberlin-Harding; 2 FE did not notify other systems; 3 FE SE/CA not used; 4 MISO did not notify other RCs; 5 MISO used non-real-time data; 6 PJM/MISO lacked seam procedures; 7 FE monitoring equipment insufficient.
- Cascade spread (p.73): Sammis-Star loss; zone 3 relays; relay/UFLS settings "not coordinated and integrated".

### NERC2004 (pp.94-97). Each cause stated to be "both a necessary condition ... and ... sufficient"

- Group 1 Causes: "FE lacked situational awareness of line outages and degraded conditions on the FE system." 1a no alarm failure detection; 1b IT did not communicate the alarm loss; 1c no full testing after failover and restore; 1d no alternative visualization; 1e no effective cyclic contingency analysis.
- Group 2 Cause: "FE did not effectively manage vegetation in its transmission rights-of-way."
- Group 3 Causes: "Reliability coordinators did not provide effective diagnostic support." 3a MISO non-real-time info (FMT/SDX); 3b no real-time topology in the MISO SE; 3c no PJM-MISO procedure.
- NERC does **not** list the TF's "Group 1: inadequate system understanding/voltage" as a cause. It lists voltage as "Other Deficiency" 8: "although voltage collapse did not initiate the sequence of events" (p.98-99). **Material inter-source difference in causal taxonomy.**
- "The causes of the blackout described here did not result from inanimate events, such as 'the alarm processor failed' or 'a tree contacted a power line.' Rather, the causes of the blackout were rooted in deficiencies resulting from decisions, actions, and the failure to act of the individuals, groups, and organizations involved." (p.94)
