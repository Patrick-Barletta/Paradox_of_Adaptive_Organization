## Q1. Decision history

### Q1(a) Re-engine the 737 vs. a new airplane (A320neo context)

- **Airbus launch.** "In December 2010, Boeing's chief competitor in the civil airplane market--the European consortium Airbus--launched the A320neo family of aircraft." The report adds that the new engines "promised up to 15-percent greater fuel efficiency over previous aircraft." -- S2 p.38. Event date: Dec 2010. CONTEMPORANEOUS event, reported RETROSPECTIVELY (2020). Confidence: high.
- **Initial Boeing position (alternative considered: new small airplane).** BCA CEO James Albaugh, 2011-01-14: "while we haven't made a firm decision," said Albaugh, "I don't think we will re-engine the 737. It's really hard to come up with a compelling business case to do that," ... "We think the right answer [is] to probably do a new small airplane that might come out toward the end of this decade." -- S2 p.38 (reconstructed from body text interleaved with a sidebar). CONTEMPORANEOUS statement. Confidence: medium (verbatim reconstructed).
- **Clean-sheet alternative.** "Since 2005, Boeing had been toying with the idea of developing a brand-new 'clean sheet' commercial airplane. Boeing dubbed these efforts ... 'Project Yellowstone.'" -- S2 p.39. CONTEMPORANEOUS fact, RETROSPECTIVE account. Confidence: high.
- **Trigger and decision.** In spring 2011 the American Airlines CEO reportedly called Boeing's CEO (S2 cites the New York Times; this is secondary inside S2). S2 then says: "Boeing instead decided to remodel its existing 737 NG aircraft so the company would not have to start from scratch, saving time, resources, and costs in the process." -- S2 p.39. On 2011-07-20, American ordered 260 A320-family aircraft "including 130 of its new A320neo aircraft" and also pledged "100 737-800s and 100 737 MAX airplanes" (S2 p.39). Boeing press release, Aug 30, 2011: "The re-engined 737 will allow Boeing to continue to deliver the most fuel efficient, most capable airplane with the lowest operating costs in the single-aisle market." -- S2 p.39. Confidence: high for the quotes; medium for the phone-call account (S2 relies on press reporting).
- **Cost of the alternatives (secondary figure inside S2).** "In 2011, the estimated cost of building a brand-new airplane hovered around $10 billion while the cost of re-engining the existing 737 NG to develop the new 737 MAX airplane was only about $3 billion, according to Richard Aboulafia with ... the Teal Group." -- S2 p.37. Confidence: medium (an analyst estimate, not a Boeing record).
- **Stated time rationale.** "In 2011, facing a competitive threat from Airbus's new, more fuel efficient, single-aisle A320 aircraft, Boeing believed it did not have time to create a new plane from scratch." -- S2 p.87. RETROSPECTIVE characterization. Confidence: medium (S2's characterization, not a quoted Boeing document).
- **Stated short-term benefits of the MAX changes.** "Collectively, the changes incorporated into the B737 MAX design resulted in increased fuel efficiency, increased range, and a reduced noise profile compared to its predecessor, the B737 NG." -- S1 p.I. Confidence: high.

### Q1(b) Engine placement and aerodynamic changes

- "the larger engines had to be mounted further forward and higher up on the wings in order to maintain sufficient ground clearance. To prevent the larger engines from dragging on the ground, the 737 MAX's nose gear was extended by about 8 inches." -- S2 p.42. The same page says these changes "altered the aerodynamics of the aircraft, making it more likely to pitch upward during certain flight conditions" (S2 pp.42-43). The 737 NG engine ground clearance is "17 inches", against "29 inches" for the 757 and "28 inches" for the 787 (S2 p.87). Event date: early in the program (no exact date given). Confidence: high.
- **Alternative first tried.** "Boeing's engineers initially considered adding small metal vanes known as vortex generators on the wings. This proposed fix did not prove to be satisfactory to address the issue, however. Ultimately, Boeing decided to add the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS)" -- S2 p.89. No date given. Confidence: medium (S2's figure source for this passage is a Seattle Times graphic).
- FAA's post-accident description: "Without the MCAS function, in some small areas of the flight envelope -- such as approaching a stall and during higher g-force maneuvering -- the new engines contribute to the control column feeling lighter in the 737 MAX than the regulations allow." and "Without MCAS, the 737 MAX would not meet FAA's regulatory requirements." -- S4 p.10. RETROSPECTIVE. Confidence: high.

### Q1(c) MCAS original design and later expansion

- **Original purpose.** "The original implementation of MCAS was driven primarily by its ability to provide the B737 MAX with FAA-compliant flight characteristics at high speed. An unaugmented design would have been at risk of not meeting 14 CFR part 25 maneuvering characteristics requirements due to aerodynamics." -- S1 p.18 (O3.4-A). Confidence: high.
- **Original authority.** "As originally designed, MCAS was only capable of moving the horizontal stabilizer a maximum of 0.6 degrees." -- S2 p.103. Confidence: high (the figure as S2 states it; S1 does not give the 0.6-degree MCAS figure. S1's "0.6 unit" refers to a different out-of-trim test condition, see Q1(e)).
- **Expansion to low speed (dated).** "Extension of MCAS to the low-speed and 1g environment during the flight program was due to unacceptable stall characteristics with STS only. The possibility of a pitch-up tendency during approach to stall was identified for the flaps-up configuration prior to the implementation of MCAS." -- S1 p.18 (O3.4-B). "in March 2016, one year prior to the FAA's certification of the 737 MAX, when Boeing test pilots found the MAX was not handling well when nearing stalls at lower speeds, Boeing redesigned MCAS to enable it to activate at lower speeds. Moreover, the new version of MCAS was capable of moving the horizontal stabilizer a maximum of 2.5 degrees (as opposed to 0.6 degrees as originally designed)." -- S2 p.103. Approval date: "On Wednesday, March 30, 2016, Boeing held a '737MAX Leadership Review' meeting" and an internal email of 2016-04-01 states "the FC [Flight Control] configuration was approved as the mitigation for basic stall characteristics" -- S2 p.104. KNKT: "As the development of the 737-8 (MAX) progressed, the MCAS function was expanded to low Mach numbers and increased to maximum MCAS command limit of 2.5 of stabilizer movement." -- S5 p.204 (Finding 1). Confidence: high.
- **Requirements revisions and safety analysis not updated.** "Boeing conducted an S&MF analysis on Revision C of the STS requirements for MCAS software, which only included high-speed values in its lookup table" (O6.9-C). "During Boeing flight tests, the company added low-speed values to the MCAS lookup table in its Revision D" (O6.9-D). "The B737-8 MAX was certified with Revision E" (O6.9-E). "The SSA was not updated beyond Revision C ... The JATR team observed no documented risk, failure, or safety analyses conducted on the MCAS software beyond Revision C." (O6.9-F). "Boeing determined the high-speed regime to be the critical aspect of MCAS, and thus no revision to the SSA was necessary when the low speeds were added" (O6.9-G). -- S1 p.34. Confidence: high.
- **Certification deliverables not updated.** "the certification plans and some certification deliverables (e.g., the preliminary system safety assessment (PSSA)) were not updated to describe the expansion of the MCAS function for the low Mach portion of the flight envelope and for compliance with stall-related requirements." -- S1 p.24 (F4.1-C). Confidence: high.
- **Comparison with the KC-46 MCAS.** On the KC-46, MCAS could move the stabilizer "only once per activation", had "limited power", used "at least two AOA sensors", and could be deactivated "by simply pulling back on the stick" -- S2 p.103. S1 F4.1-A (p.23) says MCAS "should have been considered a novelty ... owing to the important differences in function and implementation it has on the B737 MAX compared with the previous MCAS installed on the B767-C2 (tanker)." Confidence: high.
- **JATR cover letter (framing).** The letter asks "whether inadequate communications were partly responsible for the failure to address potential unintended consequences from the evolution of MCAS from a relatively benign system to a much more aggressive system" -- S1 cover letter p.2 (2019-10-11). Confidence: high.
- **Repetitive-activation issue (June 2016).** Minutes of the 2016-06-22 MCAS Review: "Some discussion on fail high AOA or fail high Mach resulting in MCAS motion. Conclusion: other systems will be reacting to the failure such as March [sic] trim or stick shaker, MCAS is small in comparison. No need to redesign to address this." and "All changes are minimal / low collateral damage, therefore no additional flight testing." -- S2 p.111. 2016-06-20 email: "I don't think it is safety, other than the pilot could fight the MCAS input and over time find themselves in a large mistrim." -- S2 p.110. CONTEMPORANEOUS (Boeing internal). Confidence: high (as quoted in S2).

### Q1(d) Reliance on a single AoA sensor

- "While every 737 MAX aircraft has two AOA sensors ..., pre-crash versions of MCAS relied on AOA data from only a single AOA sensor at a time." -- S2 p.90. "MCAS alternated which individual AOA sensor it relied upon on each flight" -- S2 p.107. Confidence: high.
- **Dated internal question (contemporaneous).** In a 2015-12-17 email, a Boeing engineer who was also an AR asked: "Are we vulnerable to single AOA sensor failures with the MCAS implementation or is there some checking that occurs?" -- S2 p.108. Confidence: high (as quoted in S2).
- **Reasoning recorded.** "Boeing's Functional Hazard Assessment regarding pilot reaction to uncommanded MCAS function did not rate the system as being hazardous enough to require redundant features, such as multiple AOA sensors." -- S2 p.107. "The MCAS architecture with redundant AOA inputs for MCAS could have been considered but was not required based on the FHA classification of Major." -- S5 p.206 (Finding 19). "If the uncommanded MCAS failure condition had been assessed as more severe than Major, the decision to rely on single AOA sensor should have been avoided." -- S5 p.206 (Finding 20). "Boeing considered that the loss of one AOA and erroneous AOA as two independent events with distinct probabilities. The combined failure event probability was assessed as beyond extremely improbable" -- S5 p.206 (Finding 15). Confidence: high.
- **JATR.** "As part of the single-channel speed trim system, the MCAS function did not include fault tolerant features, such as sensors voting or limits of authority, to limit failure effects consistent with the hazard classification." -- S1 p.30 (F6.1-A). "The AOA sensing system of the B737 MAX was not changed from the B737 NG and was not affected by any of the changes, including the MCAS." -- S1 p.8 (O1.2-A). Confidence: high.
- **Program chief engineer's knowledge.** Michael Teal acknowledged that when he approved the March 2016 redesign he was unaware "1) that MCAS operated from a single AOA sensor, 2) that MCAS could activate repeatedly, or 3) that Boeing had internal test data showing that one of its own test pilots took more than 10 seconds to react" -- S2 p.21 (interview transcript excerpt at p.117). RETROSPECTIVE testimony (2020) about a 2016 decision. Confidence: high.

### Q1(e) Hazard classification and crew-response assumptions

- **Classification.** "During the Functional Hazard Analysis (FHA), unintended MCAS-commanded stabilizer movement was considered a failure condition with Major effect in the normal flight envelope. The assessment of Major did not require Boeing to more rigorously analyze the failure condition in the safety analysis using Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) and Fault Tree Analysis (FTA), as these are only required for Hazardous or Catastrophic failure conditions." -- S5 p.204 (Finding 2). Confidence: high.
- **Possible discrepancy between sources (not reconciled here).** S1 O3.2-A (p.17) says a 1E-3 operational-flight-envelope probability was combined with the system failure probability "to achieve the 1E-7 minimum probability required for the 'hazardous' MCAS failure condition." S1 O3.3-A (p.17) says "the minimum required probability for the loss of MCAS is 1E-3 ... consistent with the 'minor' classification in the FHA." These appear to describe different failure conditions (erroneous activation vs. loss of function, and different envelopes) from the "Major" condition in S5. I did not resolve this.
- **Crew assumption (AC 25-7C).** "Boeing utilized four fundamental assumptions on crew actions in the flight control functional hazard assessment (FHA) ... The third assumption, taken from AC 25-7C, stated: 'The pilot will take immediate action to reduce or eliminate high control forces by re-trimming or changing configuration or flight conditions.'" -- S1 p.XII. Confidence: high.
- **Reaction-time assumption.** "since MCAS activation rate is 0.27 degrees of horizontal stabilizer movement per second, during the 4 seconds that it would take a pilot to respond to an erroneous activation, the stabilizer will only move a little over 1 degree" -- S1 p.14 (O2.8-C). "No studies were found that substantiate the FAA guidance concerning pilot recognition time and pilot reaction time." -- S1 p.14 (O2.8-D). Out-of-trim compliance "were found acceptable for a 0.6 unit nose-down out-of-trim condition ... determined by 3 seconds of trim input at the flaps-up main electric stabilizer trim rate of 0.2 degrees per second" and "The higher MCAS trim rate of 0.27 degrees per second was not selected for the demonstration of compliance with § 25.255" -- S1 p.19 (O3.8-A, O3.8-B). KNKT: Boeing's FHA "was also based on an assumption that the flight crew was highly reliable to respond correctly and in time within 3 seconds." -- S5 p.204 (Finding 5). Note: S1 says 4 seconds (recognition plus reaction) and S5 says 3 seconds. Confidence: high.
- **Repeated activation judged no worse than one.** "Boeing concluded that multiple erroneous MCAS activations were not worse than a single erroneous activation, based on the assumption that the crew would return the aircraft to a trimmed state (consistent with AC 25-7C guidance) following each activation." -- S1 p.34 (O6.9-H). Confidence: high.
- **Contemporaneous contrary data (Nov 2012).** On 2012-11-01 a Boeing engineer reported simulator tests where one pilot reacted in "approximately four seconds" but another found the condition "catastrophic": "The reaction time was long (>10 second) to use the aislestand (sic) stab cutout switch" -- S2 p.113. The engineer asked: "is ... appropriate to deem it hazardous and have the MCAS system designed to meet this? Or should we step up to catastrophic with the assumption that not all pilots will recognize it quickly enough?" -- S2 p.114. Coordination-sheet text repeated from 2015 to 2018: "With pilot training to recognize the runaway and use of teamwork, the failure was found Hazardous ... A typical reaction time was observed to be approximately 4 seconds. A slow reaction time scenario (>10 seconds) found the failure to be catastrophic due to the inability to arrest the airplane overspeed." -- S2 p.115. "Boeing has confirmed to the Committee that it found no record showing it shared any of these MCAS Coordination Sheets with the FAA" -- S2 p.25. CONTEMPORANEOUS documents (2012-2018), RETROSPECTIVE disclosure. Confidence: high (as quoted in S2). Note: S2 timeline p.100 says the first coordination-sheet citation was 2015-07-08.

### Q1(f) Training and simulator decisions

- **Contractual incentive (Dec 2011).** "In December 2011, Boeing agreed to pay Southwest Airlines $1 million per MAX airplane that Boeing delivered to Southwest if its pilots were unable to operate the 737 NG and 737 MAX interchangeably due to any reason." -- S2 p.139. "if Boeing failed to obtain Level B (non-simulator) training requirements or less from the FAA it would have owed Southwest between $200 to nearly $400 million." -- S2 p.24. CONTEMPORANEOUS contract, RETROSPECTIVE disclosure. Confidence: high (S2's reading of the contract; I have not seen the contract itself).
- **Design objective.** 2013-05-04 email from Michael Teal listing risk issues: "Differences Pilot Training: Ensuring that the level of change on the MAX keeps the Differences training to 16 hours or less of Level B training. Concerns include the impact of the resolution of 25.1322 trade and the Autopilot roll saturation change driven by the addition of MCAS to the flight controls system." -- S2 pp.97-98. Teal in 2020: Level B "was a design objective" -- S2 p.98. Confidence: high.
- **MCAS nomenclature (May-June 2013).** ITRACS entry, 2013-05-21: "Problem Statement: Every new buzzword represents a company and airline cost via changed manuals, changed training, changed maintenance manuals. Recommended Action: Investigate deletion of MCAS nomenclature and cover under the umbrella of 'revised speed trim'." -- S2 p.95. 2013-06-07 minutes: "If we emphasize MCAS is a new function there may be greater certification and training impact" and "This will allow us to maintain the MCAS nomenclature while not driving additional work due to training impacts and maintenance manual expansions." -- S2 pp.92, 94. Boeing's counter-position (2020-09-03 letter): "Boeing employees repeatedly disclosed the existence of MCAS, over many years, during their communications with the FAA ... identified MCAS by name and stated expressly that it was a 'new' system" -- S2 p.92. Confidence: high.
- **AEG sequencing constraint (Feb 2015).** AEG personnel said "they would not be able to make any preliminary training level determinations until after completion of T-3 Test evaluations which were not expected to happen until late 2016." Forkner: "This carries tremendous risk to the Program ... as differences greater than Level B will be unrecoverable for our early NG/MAX customers like [redacted], due to simulator availability." -- S2 p.147. CONTEMPORANEOUS. Confidence: high.
- **Cumulative-effects question settled (Jan 2016).** "the FAA raised concerns about cumulative effects of system changes from the B737 NG to the B737 MAX that may cause greater than level B differences training. Boeing's response ... was that there was no precedent in prior Boeing amended type certification projects and that AC 120-53B did not require the cumulative effects on system changes to be considered. The FAA accepted Boeing's response on 26 January 2016." -- S1 p.43 (O9.1-B). Confidence: high.
- **FCOM removal (Mar 2016).** "Information related to the MCAS functionality within the FCC originally was in the draft FCOM and was subsequently removed (around the time of MCAS Revision D, in early 2016), but without a formal process in place to ensure agreement from all disciplines" -- S1 p.47 (O10.5-A). "On March 30, 2016, hours after the MCAS redesign was approved by the 737 MAX's senior program leadership, Boeing sought, and the FAA separately approved, the removal of any references to MCAS from Boeing's Flight Crew Operations Manual (FCOM)." -- S2 p.86. "it is unclear if these actions were coordinated." -- S2 p.20. Confidence: high.
- **Level B approval.** "In August 2016, the FAA granted provisional approval for Level B (non-simulator) differences training ... The FAA estimated that its approved computer-based training for the MAX could be completed in approximately two hours" -- S2 p.26. Confidence: high.
- **Synthetic airspeed alternative rejected (2013).** Technical pilot, Feb 2013: "If synthetic airspeed is standard as opposed to an option, it would likely jeopardize the Program directive to maintain Level B training for our customers." -- S2 pp.170-171. See Q3. Confidence: high.
- **Post-Lion Air proposal and reversal.** Jan 2019: Boeing proposed Level A MCAS training (S2 p.191). FAA FSB Chair, 2019-03-01: "The FAA is concerned that software change, FCC 12.1 may not meet the definition of Level A differences ... we are advising the Boeing Company that the evaluation is proceeding at risk." -- S2 p.217. 2020-01-07 Boeing statement: "Boeing is recommending 737 MAX simulator training in addition to computer based training for all MAX pilots prior to return to service of the 737 MAX." -- S2 p.161. Confidence: high.

### Q1(g) Certification approach: amended TC and delegation

- **Dates.** "Boeing applied for certification of the B737-8 MAX ... on June 30, 2012, and the FAA certified the aircraft on March 8, 2017." -- S1 p.I fn.1. S2 gives "January 27, 2012" for the initial ATC proposal and "March 9, 2017" for the ATC on p.41, but "March 8, 2017" on p.5. **Internal inconsistency in S2; S1 and S2 p.5 agree on March 8.** Confidence: high for S1; flag S2 p.41.
- **Significant change; exceptions.** FAA, 2012-07-21: "[T]he FAA has found that the proposed Model 737-8 is a significant product level change. Thus, the changed product must comply with the regulations in effect on the date of application for the change unless an exception given in § 21.101(b) applies" -- S2 p.44. The G-1 Issue Paper listed "201 distinct 'significant changes'" and "briefly cited MCAS as one of the software changes" -- S2 p.43. FAA, 2014-02-06: "Boeing stated that a major reason for not stepping up to the latest amendment is to minimize the impact of changes in the flight deck and maintain a common flight deck philosophy with the 737 fleet of airplanes. Boeing also asserted that updating the 737-8 with an engine-indicating and crew alerting system [EICAS] type system will have a major impact on the type rating and training difference level" -- S2 pp.45-46. Exceptions were listed for "about 40 regulations" -- S1 p.11 (F1.9-A). Confidence: high.
- **JATR on the Changed Product Rule.** "the Changed Product Rule process was followed and that the process was effective for addressing discrete changes. However, the team determined that the process did not adequately address cumulative effects, system integration, and human factors issues." -- S1 p.IV. "Boeing's compliance submissions to the FAA followed a bottom-up approach whereby each change and areas affected by a change were presented separately" -- S1 p.8 (F1.1-C). "some elements of the design and certification remain rooted in the original 1967 certification ... The basic federated architecture of the B737 has remained largely unchanged ... and drove many design decisions more than 50 years after it was originally designed." -- S1 p.7 (F1.1-A). Confidence: high.
- **Delegation.** "The FAA initially delegated acceptance of approximately 40% of the B737 MAX project's certification plans to the Boeing ODA. Additional certification plans that were originally retained ... were later delegated" -- S1 p.26 (O5.1-A). "Safety critical areas, including system safety documents related to MCAS, were initially retained by the FAA and then delegated to the Boeing ODA." -- S1 p.26 (F5.1-A). "by November 2016 ... the FAA had delegated 79 of 91 certification plans to Boeing" -- S2 p.101. Stabilizer SSA: on 2015-04-14 the FAA said it "would be retained by the FAA"; in Dec 2016 the FAA marked it "'delegated SSA approval to ODA.'" -- S2 p.101 (quoting NTSB). "The Boeing ODA organization is staffed by approximately 1,500 people, whereas the FAA's BASOO is staffed by 45 people." -- S1 p.27 (O5.2-B). "FHA/ PSSA information being submitted much too late (at type inspection authorization) for the FAA to have any influence on the proposed MCAS design" -- S1 p.24 (F4.3-A). Confidence: high.

## Q2. Did a modification ADD a mandatory sequential step? Remediation ordering and duration

### Q2.1 Evidence that the original MCAS design built a repeated crew-action step into recovery (in-flight)

- KNKT on the delivered design: MCAS inputs "could be stopped or reversed by the pilots using their yoke-mounted electric stabilizer trim switches, which also reset the system after a 5 second delay. The latter behavior is based on the assumption that flight crews use the trim switches to completely return the aircraft to neutral trim. In the FCC software version current at the time of the accident, if the original elevated AOA condition persists for more than 5 seconds following an MCAS flight control law reset, the MCAS flight control law will command another stabilizer nose down trim input" -- S5 p.49. RETROSPECTIVE (2019) description of a 2016-2017 design. Confidence: high.
- "If the flight crew uses manual electric trim, the MCAS resets. That means 5 seconds, the system can make another AND command requiring the flight crew to respond again." ... "To recover, the flight crew could: 1) stop making manual electric trim inputs (which would stop resetting MCAS), or 2) make sufficient (long duration) manual electric trim inputs to return the aircraft to a trimmed state, or 3) use the stabilizer trim cutout procedure to stop all electric trim commands and control trim using the manual trim wheels. If one of these three flight crew trim responses is not used, a subsequent combination of MCAS commands and short-duration (insufficient) flight crew electric trim inputs may result in a mis-trimmed condition that cannot be controlled." -- S5 pp.193-194. Confidence: high.
- Accumulation of mis-trim: "the control column force to maintain level flight could eventually increase to a level where control forces alone may not be adequate to control the aircraft. The cumulative mis-trim could not be countered by using elevator alone which is contrary to the Boeing assumption during FHA." -- S5 p.205 (Finding 9). The KNKT simulator found "after just two activations of MCAS, absent any counter from the pilot, the control column force became 'too heavy' to move." -- S2 p.116 (quoting S5). Column forces "exceeded 100 pounds, which is more than the 75-pound limit" -- S5 p.206 (Finding 21). Confidence: high.
- FAA's post-accident framing of the same issue: "Safety Item #2: MCAS RESET GENERATES REPETITIVE MCAS COMMANDS ... the MCAS control law uses pilot release of the electric trim switch to reset MCAS activation. Once reset, the MCAS system will make another airplane nose-down stabilizer trim command after five seconds." and "Safety Item #3: ... multiple MCAS commands resulted in a significant horizontal stabilizer mistrim condition, which the flightcrew could not counter using only elevator control." -- S4 pp.7-8 and 20. Confidence: high.
- Timing note: S1 O3.4-B and S2 p.103 date the low-speed and 2.5-degree expansion to March 2016. None of the sources I read states *when* the reset/repeat-activation logic was introduced or whether it changed with Revision D. **Not found.**

### Q2.2 Evidence on sequential steps added by design/certification choices (pre-accident)

- **EICAS as a cascading-dependency argument (Boeing, June 2012).** The Boeing presentation "737 MAX Certification Basis Risk Review: EICAS" (June 14, 2012 per S2 sidebar) said: "IF we had to comply outright with regulation: Considerable program cost and schedule risk" and "significant impact" on pilot training; "Boeing's exception proposal will be based on compliance with the regulation being 'impractical.'" ... "cost of complying not commensurate with degree of safety improvement"; and it ended: "This would have cascading effects on flight deck design and other systems." -- S2 pp.46-47. CONTEMPORANEOUS. This is the clearest contemporaneous statement in the record that one change (EICAS) would force downstream changes; the record does not describe it as adding a sequential recovery step. Confidence: high (as quoted in S2).
- **Training-determination ordering.** The AEG said it could not make "preliminary training level determinations until after completion of T-3 Test evaluations which were not expected to happen until late 2016" -- S2 p.147 (Feb 2015). This is an ordering constraint in the original certification, not a safety-recovery step. CONTEMPORANEOUS. Confidence: high.
- **S2 "cascading effect" language (RETROSPECTIVE, interpretive).** "More than any other program objective, ensuring that the FAA's pilot training requirements for the MAX did not include simulator training had an incredibly significant cascading effect on the 737 MAX program that undermined the safety of the flying public." -- S2 p.141. Confidence: high that the quote is accurate; it is the Committee staff's interpretation.

### Q2.3 Remediation (2019-2020): documented duration

- "This thorough review has taken more than 22 months and included the full-time work of more than 40 engineers, inspectors, pilots, and technical support staff. The effort represents more than 60,000 FAA hours of review, certification testing, and evaluation of pertinent documents. This includes approximately 50 hours of FAA flight or simulator tests and FAA analysis of more than 4,000 hours of company flight and simulator testing." -- S4 p.5 (repeated p.35). Event span: approximately Nov 2018-Nov 2020. RETROSPECTIVE. Confidence: high.
- Grounding: Emergency Order of Prohibition 2019-03-13 (S1 p.I; S4 p.19). AD 2020-24-02 effective 2020-11-20 (S3 p.74560). The interval from grounding to AD is about 20 months (my arithmetic, not a figure stated in the sources).
- **Early expectations vs. actual duration.** Boeing official to APA, 2018-11-27: fixes would be finalized "in a fairly short period of time; weeks, not -- not -- not a year, but a couple -- maybe six weeks-ish" -- S2 p.205. The FAA's Dec 2018 TARAM "estimated that Boeing would have a fix for MCAS by July 2019" -- S2 pp.210-211 (citing DOT OIG). "Despite optimistic predictions at the time--that a simple software fix for MCAS would allow the 737 MAX to return quickly to service--the aircraft has been grounded for 18 months, with even more, newly discovered safety issues emerging since." -- S2 p.12. Confidence: high (as quoted in S2).
- **Scope growth during remediation (documented).** "Boeing's initial efforts focused on changing the activation and reset criteria for the MCAS control laws ... As a result of continuous review and testing of Boeing's proposed software design changes, the FAA took a broader approach to how MCAS functions within the Speed Trim System (STS). The result is an extensive set of improvements to MCAS" -- S4 p.9 (also p.19). "As a result of the design work to change the MCAS behavior and the subsequent review of the Integrated Speed Trim System Safety Analysis, the FAA required an additional evaluation ... The FAA evaluated Boeing's determination of a non-compliance with FAA wire separation requirements. As a result, Boeing developed changes to the system, which include physical separation of existing wires" -- S4 p.11. TAB item: "Boeing to address the FCC CPU and memory postulated failure issue discovered during eCAB certification testing" closed with an integrated SSA "dated August 25, 2020" -- S4 p.54. S2 p.223-224: Jan 2020 FCC power-up failure after the updated software was installed; April 2020 further FCC software updates for two separate issues. S2 calls these "additional barriers to the airplane's safe return to service" (S2 p.223). Confidence: high.

### Q2.4 Remediation: documented ordering of required steps

**Fleet-level (FAA) gating, sequential as written:**
- "The FAA rescinded the Emergency Order of Prohibition to allow the 737 MAX to return to service based on completion of the following actions: [bullet] FAA approval of the amended aircraft design. [bullet] Issuance of the AD that mandates specific actions that owners or operators must take to correct the unsafe condition. Additionally, the following conditions are prerequisites to resuming operations in the U.S.: [bullet] For previously delivered 737 MAX airplanes, owner/operators must incorporate all FAA-mandated design changes and maintenance actions. ... All US air-carrier pilots must complete the 737 MAX special training described in the 737 FSB report before serving in a 737 MAX airplane." -- S4 pp.13-14. Confidence: high.
- TAB as a gate: "All findings that the TAB members identified as required for return to service of the 737 MAX were resolved to their satisfaction." -- S3 p.74561. "The TAB also reviewed the final Boeing submission and issue a final report prior to a final determination of compliance by the FAA." -- S4 p.54. Confidence: high.
- Generic certification ordering (FAA description): "Once the FAA accepts this plan and either retains or delegates approval of the project, compliance activities can begin." ... "When the applicant submits all compliance data deliverables and the FAA or its designee approve them, the project is closed and the change is certified. Then, the certification is approved as airworthy when installed on an airplane" -- S4 p.36. Confidence: high.
- FSB/training chain: JOEB evaluation concluded 2020-09-22; draft FSB Rev 17 posted 2020-10-06; comments closed 2020-11-02; final FSB Rev 17 published 2020-11-18 (S4 p.11; S3 p.74560-74561 gives the final FSB date as November 16, 2020). **Minor date discrepancy (Nov 16 in S3 vs Nov 18 in S4).** S3 says the AD "does not mandate pilot training", but "in accordance with 14 CFR 121.405(e), the FAA is requiring air carriers to revise all Boeing 737 MAX training curricula to include the special training as described in the 737 FSB Report." -- S3 p.74573. Confidence: high.

**Per-airplane (AD 2020-24-02) ordering, verbatim:**
- (g) FCC software: "Before further flight, install FCC OPS software version P12.1.2 ... on FCC A and FCC B, and do a software installation verification." -- S3 p.74584.
- (h) AFM: "Before further flight, revise the existing AFM to include the changes specified in paragraphs (h)(1) through (10)" -- S3 p.74585.
- (j) MDS software (AOA DISAGREE), (k) stabilizer trim wire routing, (l) AOA sensor system test: each begins "Before further flight, do all applicable actions identified as 'RC'..." -- S3 p.74592.
- (m)(1) Operational readiness flight: "After accomplishment of all applicable required actions in paragraphs (g) through (l) of this AD, do all applicable actions identified as 'RC' for the 'Operational Readiness Flight' ... The 'Operational Readiness Flight' required by this paragraph must be accomplished before any other flight." (m)(2): "After the 'Operational Readiness Flight' and before further flight, any mechanical irregularities that occurred during the 'Operational Readiness Flight' must be resolved" -- S3 p.74592.
- FAA rationale for the ORF ordering: "The FAA did not intend the reference to 'before further flight' in paragraph (m)(1) of this AD to include the ORF. Therefore, the FAA has revised paragraph (m)(1) ... to require the ORF to be completed 'before any other flight.' The FAA finds that completion of the actions specified in paragraphs (g) through (l) of this AD is adequate to accomplish the ORF safely." -- S3 p.74579.
- **Sequential vs. parallel (as documented):** The AD sets an explicit order only between the group (g)-(l) and (m): (g)-(l), then ORF, then resolve irregularities. Within (g)-(l), each is "before further flight" and **no order among them is specified** (so they are not documented as sequential with respect to each other). Paragraph (o) gives credit for MDS (j) and wiring (k) actions "performed before the effective date of this AD" using June 2020 service bulletins. That shows some per-airplane work could be, and was permitted to be, done before the AD and in parallel with final certification. -- S3 p.74592.
- Per-airplane effort (FAA cost table): FCC software 1 work-hour; AFM 1 work-hour; MDS 1 work-hour; wiring "Up to 79 work-hours"; AOA sensor system test "10 work-hours"; "no definitive data" for the ORF -- S3 p.74584. Boeing revised the AOA test estimate from 40 to 10 work-hours (S3 p.74579). Confidence: high.
- Parallel activity during certification: "the FAA supported multiple concurrent validation activities with four foreign authorities" -- S4 p.35. "The FAA also actively supported concurrent validation activities of the aircraft by the CAAs of other States of Design" -- S4 p.6. Confidence: high.
- Production aircraft: the AD does not apply to undelivered airplanes "because the new design is approved as part of the baseline type-certificated design and incorporates all the changes prior to first flight and approval." -- S4 p.56. Confidence: high.

### Q2.5 What the remediation removed (design changes that reduce dependence on crew steps)

- "The new flight control laws now require inputs from both AOA sensors in order to activate MCAS ... if those inputs differ significantly (greater than 5.5 degrees for a specified period of time), will disable the Speed Trim System (STS), which includes MCAS, for the remainder of the flight ... The new flight control laws now permit only one activation of MCAS per sensed high-AOA event, and limit the magnitude of any MCAS command ... such that the resulting position of the stabilizer will preserve the flightcrew's ability to control the airplane's pitch by using only the control column. This means the pilot will have sufficient control authority without the need to make electric or manual stabilizer trim inputs." -- S3 p.74561. "The FAA concluded ... that no STS failure modes rely on exceptional piloting skills or immediate pilot actions." -- S4 p.13. RETROSPECTIVE. Confidence: high.
- Counterpoint inside the remediation: it added eight new or revised non-normal procedures (S4 p.11) and a one-time full-flight-simulator "special training" (S4 p.55; S3 p.74560). The TAB recorded that the one-time training "was proposed by Boeing with consideration that the 737 MAX had been out of service for over one year and does not change the differences level determination" -- S4 p.55. Confidence: high.

### Q2 summary of what is NOT documented
- I found no source that describes an original design modification as adding a mandatory step that had to be completed *before a fix could be certified*. The closest items are (i) the AEG's statement that training-level determination had to wait for T-3 tests (S2 p.147) and (ii) the TAB/FAA gating of RTS (S4 pp.13, 54).
- I found no document that times the remediation stages against each other (for example, how long software certification took versus training development). S4 gives only the aggregate ">22 months".

## Q4. Evidence against an accumulating-dependency account (attributions to specific defects, oversight, communication, schedule/cost)

- **KNKT contributing factors (all nine).** These are framed as individual design, assumption, maintenance and crew-performance factors: "1. During the design and certification of the Boeing 737-8 (MAX), assumptions were made about flight crew response to malfunctions which, even though consistent with current industry guidelines, turned out to be incorrect. 2. Based on the incorrect assumptions about flight crew response and an incomplete review of associated multiple flight deck effects, MCAS's reliance on a single sensor was deemed appropriate and met all certification requirements. 3. MCAS was designed to rely on a single AOA sensor, making it vulnerable to erroneous input from that sensor. 4. The absence of guidance on MCAS or more detailed use of trim in the flight manuals and in flight crew training, made it more difficult for flight crews to properly respond to uncommanded MCAS. 5. The AOA DISAGREE alert was not correctly enabled during Boeing 737-8 (MAX) development. ... 6. The replacement AOA sensor ... had been mis-calibrated during an earlier repair. ... 7. The investigation could not determine that the installation test of the AOA sensor was performed properly. ... 8. Lack of documentation in the aircraft flight and maintenance log about the continuous stick shaker and use of the Runaway Stabilizer NNC ... 9. The multiple alerts, repetitive MCAS activations, and distractions related to numerous ATC communications were not able to be effectively managed. ... These performances had previously been identified during training and reappeared during the accident flight." -- S5 p.215. The report says the order "is based on chronological order and not to show the degree of contribution." RETROSPECTIVE. Confidence: high.
- **House executive summary (defects, management and oversight).** "Technical design flaws, faulty assumptions about pilot responses, and management failures by both The Boeing Company (Boeing) and the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) played instrumental and causative roles in the chain of errors" -- S2 p.5. "They were the horrific culmination of a series of faulty technical assumptions by Boeing's engineers, a lack of transparency on the part of Boeing's management, and grossly insufficient oversight by the FAA--the pernicious result of regulatory capture" -- S2 p.6. The five themes are production pressures, faulty design and performance assumptions, culture of concealment, conflicted representation, and Boeing's influence over FAA oversight (S2 pp.12-14). Note: S2 also uses "series"/"chain" language, which can be read either way. Confidence: high.
- **Schedule/cost pressure.** "There was tremendous financial pressure on Boeing and the 737 MAX program to compete with Airbus' new A320neo aircraft ... this pressure resulted in extensive efforts to cut costs, maintain the 737 MAX program schedule, and avoid slowing the 737 MAX production line." -- S2 pp.12-13. Countdown clock: "One of the mantras that we had was the value of a day" (Leverkuhn) -- S2 p.17. Confidence: high.
- **Communication / oversight failures (JATR).** "the FAA had inadequate awareness of the MCAS function which, coupled with limited involvement, resulted in an inability of the FAA to provide an independent assessment" -- S1 p.VII. "The FAA was not completely unaware of MCAS; however, because the information and discussions about MCAS were so fragmented and were delivered to disconnected groups within the process, it was difficult to recognize the impacts and implications of this system." -- S1 p.13 (F2.7-A). "signs were reported of undue pressures on Boeing ODA engineering unit members" -- S1 p.VII. Confidence: high.
- **Design-process defects (JATR).** "the design process was not sufficient to identify all the potential MCAS hazards" (F6.1-A); "The use of pilot action as a primary mitigation means for MCAS hazards ... is not in accordance with Boeing's process instructions for safe design" (F6.1-B); "An integrated SSA to investigate the MCAS as a complete function was not performed." (F6.5-A); "The Boeing analysis of erroneous MCAS activation did not adequately take into account what else might be happening at the same time" (F6.7-A) -- S1 pp.30-33. Confidence: high.
- **No noncompliance found (cuts against "defect" framing too).** Boeing's Dec 2018 presentation listed items, each with "This did not entail a 'process violation or non-compliance.'" -- S2 p.209. FAA draft BASOO review (last version 2019-02-08): "The oversight activity did not reveal any noncompliances ... but did observe some assumptions used by the Applicant and accepted by the FAA." -- S2 p.31. JATR: "the Changed Product Rule process was followed" -- S1 p.IV. Confidence: high (draft report quoted in S2; S2 staff reviewed it in camera).
- **Crew and maintenance factors raised in the record.** Lion Air captain had "over 5,100 hours" on 737s and first officer "more than 4,200 hours" (S2 p.10). JATR F9.3-A notes training and licensing differences across CAAs (S1 p.44). The FAA RTS conclusion says the report "does not address other safety issues that might have contributed to the accidents but are not related to airplane design. This includes the airline maintenance practices, the aircraft operators' compliance posture, and pilot training effectiveness." -- S4 p.14. Confidence: high.
- **FAA post-Lion Air view of crew capability.** "The FAA conclusion from this analysis indicated that an average crew with no prior awareness could sort out the failure scenario and could correctly mitigate the failure using existing procedures." -- S4 p.18 (describing the FAA's Nov 2018 analysis of the prior Lion Air flight). FAA Associate Administrator in 2019: "the flight deck philosophy design was based on pilot intervention and action." -- S2 p.205. Confidence: high.
- **Explicit absence.** None of S1, S2, S4 or S5 describes the accidents as caused by an accumulation of sequential recovery steps that had to be completed in order. S5 Findings 9 and 13 describe accumulating *mis-trim* within a flight when the crew did not take one of three recovery actions (see Q2.1).
