# Case D: 2007–2009 financial crisis. Data-availability audit and evidence extraction

Scope: collecting evidence only. No hypothesis is graded here. Evidence for and against appears in each section, and gaps are stated explicitly.
Hypothesis under test (not assumed): protective margins set from recent experience were relaxed during long calm periods before 2007, which left less protection for later shocks.
Compiled: 2026-11-22. All quotes below were extracted verbatim from PDFs downloaded during this session with `pdftotext`. Where a quote contains "[...]", text was omitted. Page cites give the **printed page** and, in parentheses, the PDF page.

Tags: **CONTEMPORANEOUS** means the data or publication was available before mid-2007. **RETROSPECTIVE** means it appeared later. Confidence levels: H = verbatim from the primary document; M = verbatim, but interpretation or alignment is uncertain; L = indirect.

## Pre-registered adversarial check (done first): tri-party margins stable 2007–2009?

**Result: confirmed, with a coverage caveat that is central.** Both FRBNY reports find that tri-party margins were stable for most borrowers. Their data, however, **begins 1 July 2008**, after Bear Stearns. Neither report covers any part of the pre-2007 calm period, or Aug 2007–Jun 2008.

- S2, Abstract (PDF p.2): "Using confidential data on tri-party repo, a major segment of this market, we show that the level of margins and the amount of funding were surprisingly stable for most borrowers during the crisis. However, we also document a sharp decline in the tri-party repo funding of Lehman in September 2008." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
- S1, Abstract (PDF p.2): "We also use data from July 2008 to early 2010 to document quantitative features of he market. We find that both the level of haircuts and the amount of funding were surprisingly stable in this market. The stability of the margins is in contrast to evidence from other repo markets." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
- S1, p.16 fn.14 (PDF p.18): "Some of the most dramatic events in the tri-party repo market occurred between September 2007 and July 2008, in particular before March 17, 2008, when the PDCF was introduced. Unfortunately, we do not have data covering that period." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
- S2, p.12 (PDF p.14): "Our data begin after the fall of Bear Stearns but before Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy." (H)

What this rules in and rules out: the check shows that tri-party margins did not spiral during Jul 2008–Jan 2010. It **cannot** show whether tri-party margins were lowered during 2003–2007, because no tri-party margin data before July 2008 appears in either report.

This is in stark contrast with the study of Gorton and Metrick (2009) of an interdealer market for less liquid collateral and the experience of some other repo markets. The stability of haircuts in tri-party repo seems to be due, in part, to the behavior of some large cash investors who look at the counterparty to a repo first and to the collateral second." (H)
2. S2 p.2 (PDF 4), by collateral class: "In the tri-party repo market, however, margins for all but the lowest-quality collateral changed very little [...] margins for repos collateralized by U.S. Treasuries and agency debentures did not change throughout the crisis. (H. Table text extracted cleanly.)

5. S1 p.46 (PDF 48): "we find that the median haircuts for each asset type barely increased during the financial crisis (see Figure 7). For the three asset groups that make up the majority of collateral posted in this market - Treasuries, Agency Debentures and Agency MBS - haircuts hardly moved over the second half of the 2008." (H)
6. Some movement did occur in tails and non-government classes. S1 p.49 (PDF 51): "Looking at non-Fed-eligible collateral (Figure 11), we see the classic reaction to financial distress. Starting a month or so after the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy, the spread in haircuts dramatically expanded. The increase in the haircut spread was maintained through January 2009, before narrowing to its pre-Lehman Brothers bankruptcy levels." 7. Economic size of the change. S1 p.67 (PDF 69): a $257bn hypothetical portfolio "would have raised $250.2 billion in cash" at Jul–Aug 2008 average haircuts vs "$249.1 billion" at Sep–Dec 2008 haircuts, "about $1.1 billion less. 8. Borrower heterogeneity. S1 p.24 (PDF 26): "there is substantial heterogeneity in the haircuts dealers face, even for highly liquid assets such as US Treasuries. Second, some dealers face high haircuts relative to their peers across all asset classes." Further, one dealer is forced to pay a relatively high margin of 5%. [...] it is typically the smaller dealers that face the higher margins." (H)
9. Counterparty risk vs margins. S2 p.30 (PDF 32): "a one percentage change in a dealer's CDS is correlated with a 0.003 percentage change in margins, a negligible amount." S2 p.31 (PDF 33): "investors do not react to adverse information by changing margins, requesting better collateral, or ratcheting down the amount they lend to dealers. The exception to this finding is, of course, Lehman Brothers. [...] adjustments in funding may be highly nonlinear." (H)
10. Lehman (borrower-specific). S2 Table VI, p.26 (PDF 28): Lehman's margin spread over other large dealers was −0.05 (July avg), 0.48 (Sep 10), 0.66 (Sep 11), 1.11 (Sep 12) and 4.11 (Sep 15). ## Q2. Bilateral vs tri-party segments

1. Data used for the bilateral side. S2 p.20–21 (PDF 22–23): "we use confidential daily survey data on margins of bilateral repos obtained from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. In this segment of the repo market (segment 5 in Figure 3), dealers provide liquidity to hedge funds, real estate investment trusts, and banks". S1 p.67 (PDF 69) describes the bilateral haircuts as "haircuts from a survey of hedge funds". Figure 2 (S2) and Figure 26 (S1) start in **Jul-08**. Neither report gives a pre-2008 bilateral series. (RETROSPECTIVE, H. The coverage start is inferred from figure axes: M.)
2. Divergence. S2 p.21–22 (PDF 23–24): "The median margin is higher in the bilateral repo market for all collateral classes except Treasuries. After the Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy, these margin spreads increased, reaching more than 40 percentage points for subprime collateral. In general, the margin spreads increased during the fall of 2008, peaked sometime in the first half of 2009, and were close to their July 2008 level at the beginning of 2010." S2 p.2 (PDF 4) adds that spreads "are sizable for corporate bonds (over 10 percentage points in 2009) and enormous for private-label collateralized mortgage obligations and asset-backed securities (over 30 percentage points in 2009)." (H)
3. Segment map and which study covers which segment. S2 p.7–8 (PDF 9–10): "Gorton and Metrick (2012) use margin data from a dealer operating in the bilateral interdealer repo market (segment 4). The bilateral data we study in this paper are from dealers lending to their clients (segment 5). Our paper focuses on the tri-party repo market and, more specifically, on segment 2. [...] We are not aware of any data source that covers segment 3 explicitly." (H)
4. Relative size, July–August 2008. S2 p.7 (PDF 9): "the sum of all repos outstanding on a typical day [...] represented approximately $6.1 trillion. About 40% of this activity is tri-party repo, while the remaining 60% is bilateral repos." App. A (PDF 47) gives the method: FR 2004 primary dealer repos of $4.0 trillion, inflated. (H. Note that S1 p.4 (PDF 6) says bilateral DvP repos "are believed to represent a small share of dealer funding". The two FRBNY reports characterise bilateral size differently.)
5. Explanations offered (conjectural, by the authors' own account). S2 p.33–34 (PDF 35–36): "some of the cash lenders in the tri-party repo market appear reluctant or unprepared to take possession of the collateral. Consequently, these cash lenders prefer to withdraw funding rather than increase margins". In bilateral markets, "dealers are the cash lenders. Dealers are adept at financing and liquidating collateral". A "monopoly rents" prime-brokerage hypothesis also appears in S2 p.33 (PDF 35) and S1 p.66 (PDF 68). S2 p.34 (PDF 36): "More work, including the gathering of better data across repo market segments, needs to be done to convincingly advance the hypothesis". (H)
6. Run dynamics. S2 p.35 (PDF 37): "The behavior of margins in the bilateral repo market can be consistent with a market-wide run [...] In contrast, the tri-party repo market exhibited precipitous reductions in the tri-party repo funding of specific institutions, something more similar to traditional bank runs." (H)
7. Data gap. S2 p.40 (PDF 42): "the lack of data is a serious limitation for researchers interested in understanding the repo market. In the U.S., the most glaring need is data on the bilateral market." (H)

## Q4. Calm-conditioned setting and relaxation of margins and limits

### 4a. Statements that margins/limits were set from recent volatility or experience (supporting the mechanism)
1. **CGFS 36 (S7) p.3 (PDF 13)**, haircut-setting method: "Key quantitative factors include value-at-risk (VaR), measures of the liquidity of the collateral asset and – on a more ad hoc basis – stress tests. Qualitative factors include type of counterparty, competitive pressures and client relations. The historical time period used in determining VaR-based haircuts and margins is usually one year, but longer time periods (up to five years) were also reported as being used." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
2. **CGFS 36 p.10 (PDF 20)**: "Practical experience over the last few years suggests that practices for setting credit terms such as haircuts and initial margins have indeed been procyclical. During the years of economic expansion prior to mid-2007, there was a gradual erosion of risk management standards applied to secured lending. Haircuts fell to low levels, and other credit terms were loosened in response to competitive pressures. This allowed a build-up of leverage inside and outside the regulated sector." (RETROSPECTIVE, H. Qualitative. No dated series is cited.)
3. **CGFS 36 Exec. summary p.vii (PDF 7)**: "High credit ratings, ample liquidity and low financial market volatility during this period increased the level of comfort of borrowers with their reliance on secured funding, and the comfort of lenders with, in many cases, modest and declining haircuts. The gradual erosion of lending terms during the period of high liquidity and low volatility was abruptly reversed when market conditions deteriorated." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
4. **CGFS 36 p.10 (PDF 20)**, mechanism: "To the extent that lower credit spreads are perceived as reflecting lower liquidity risk premiums and/or lower default risk expectations, haircut levels and other requirements are likely to be reduced in response. Relaxation of terms may encourage higher leverage, which in turn increases asset prices and lowers asset price volatility. As a consequence, risk measures derived using these variables as inputs will be distorted." (RETROSPECTIVE, H. Stated as a mechanism, not as an observed series.)
5. **Task Force (S6) §6, 22 of 43 (PDF 47)**: "Stress testing: In cases of stable markets where implied volatility is low and historical volatility assumptions have decayed, an overlay of market stress testing to determine margin levels is critical to ensure that a low volatility environment does not lead to pro-cyclical behavior." Also: "market participants should complement a historical volatility analysis with the implied volatility in the markets. This is important since history is not always a good proxy for the future." (RETROSPECTIVE, H. Normative and forward-looking. It implies prior reliance on historical volatility but does not document it.)
6. **Task Force (S6), Summary, 4 of 43 (PDF 29)**: "Margining Practices – Market participants in many cases did not anticipate the extent to which market conditions could worsen and did not set margins accordingly, leading to pro-cyclical increases in those margins when conditions did worsen during the crisis." 5 of 43 (PDF 30): "Margin pro-cyclicality refers to the process by which margin levels are reduced in good times and increased in bad times." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
7. **FRBNY White Paper (S5) p.14 (PDF 15)**: "During normal times, competitive dynamics and an abundance of market liquidity led investors and clearing banks to adopt liberal policies on collateral eligibility, the size and concentration of portfolios, and haircuts. During times of financial stress, the desire by investors and clearing banks to protect themselves can lead to sudden withdrawals of credit or sharp increases in margins and haircuts." p.3 (PDF 4): risk management practices "that were, with the benefit of hindsight, clearly inadequate and vulnerable to procyclical pressures". (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
8. **SSG (S9) p.14 (PDF 19)**, internal risk limits/VaR: "some firms' initial assessment of the true potential losses they faced were likely skewed downward by their VaR measures' underlying assumptions and a dependence on historical data from more benign periods. Firms suggested that VaR calculations based on new market data were anywhere from about 10 percent to at least 200 percent higher compared with VaR calculations conducted using data sets reflecting earlier, and more favorable, market conditions. The increase in most firms' VaR calculations ranged from 30 percent to 80 percent. Firms reported between two and sixteen back-testing exceptions during the third quarter of 2007 alone." (RETROSPECTIVE, survey Nov 2007 / pub. Mar 2008, H)
9. **SSG p.5 (PDF 10)**: "some firms that encountered more substantial challenges tended to assume that they could apply the low historical return volatility of corporate credits rated Aaa to super-senior tranches of CDOs [...] That assumption turned out to be wrong". p.1 (PDF 6): "Because these and other innovative products had been created during the prior period of more benign market conditions, banks and securities firms had not observed how such products would behave during a significant market downturn". (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
10. **ECB FSR June 2007 (S10) p.112–113 (PDF 113–114)**, CONTEMPORANEOUS: "competition among prime broker banks for the lucrative hedge fund servicing business remains intense, thereby undermining the effectiveness of counterparty discipline exercised by banks, and leaving them more vulnerable to adverse market shocks. The reduced downside protection is not confined to the less frequent use of initial margins at the inception of various OTC trades, but also refers to more aggressive VaR-based cross-product (portfolio) margining practices and looser collateral policies involving the wider use of lower-grade and potentially less liquid collateral. VaR measures may prove particularly inadequate should correlations between financial instruments increase, and if volatility rises from unusually subdued levels." (CONTEMPORANEOUS, June 2007, a borderline mid-2007 date. H)
11. **BoE FSR April 2007 (S11) p.38 (PDF 36)**, CONTEMPORANEOUS: "Market contacts suggest that competition for prime brokerage accounts has encouraged some brokers to take on greater market risk in order to preserve or gain market share and future earnings. For example, there is evidence of an erosion of lending terms, particularly of initial margins." (CONTEMPORANEOUS, H. The underlying evidence is not shown.)
12. **IMF GFSR Apr 2008 (S8) Table 1.2 p.23**, early-2007 vs April-2008 snapshot (selected rows, raw-mode extraction): U.S. treasuries 0.25 → 3; Investment-grade bonds 0–3 → 8–12; High-yield bonds 10–15 → 25–40; Equities 15 → 20; ABS CDOs AAA 2–4 → 15; AAA CLO 4 → 10–20; AAA RMBS 2–4 → 10–20; Alt-A MBS 3–5 → 20–50. "Sources: Citigroup; and IMF staff estimates." Footnote 34, p.19: hedge funds "had negotiated 'margin locks' that prevented their prime brokers from increasing the margins [...] for a fixed period of time." (RETROSPECTIVE, M. Values are from a raw text extraction, and the layout extraction misaligned some rows. These are levels at one early-2007 point, not a decline over a calm period.)
13. **CGFS 36 Table 1 p.2 (PDF 12)**, June 2007 vs June 2009, prime / non-prime / unrated counterparties (selected): Prime MBS AAA 4/6/10 → 10/20/30–100; ABS 10/20/20 → 25/50/100; Structured products (AAA) 10/15/20 → 100/100/100; G7 govt short-term 0/0/0.5 → 0.5/1/2. ### 4b. Evidence that complicates or contradicts the calm-conditioned mechanism
1. **CGFS 36 p.4 (PDF 14)**: "Portfolio margining models, often used in prime brokerage, might have been expected to generate volatile margins that responded to changes in market volatility and correlation. However, the majority of such models appear to have used volatility assumptions backed out from historical stressed periods rather than the most recent data so that, for more liquid asset classes such as G7 government bonds and equities, haircuts changed only modestly." (RETROSPECTIVE, H. This runs directly against "set from recent experience" for those models and asset classes.)
2. **CGFS 36 p.4 (PDF 14)**: the stated driver of pre-crisis haircut levels is **competition**, not volatility inputs: "Prior to the crisis, competitive pressures influenced the level of haircuts in some business areas. Indeed, competitive pressure had been particularly strong in firms that relied on securities financing as a major source of revenue, or in business lines (such as prime brokerage) that used lower haircuts to attract business." Exec. summary p.vii: "Competitive pressures have a strong influence on securities financing haircuts and the range of eligible collateral for such transactions in good times." (H. This is a competing mechanism for relaxation that is not calm-conditioned.)
3. **CGFS 36 p.vii (PDF 7)**, drivers of crisis increases: "Reassessment of the market liquidity of collateral assets and counterparty credit quality, as well as higher volatility, were the main factors that drove those increases." p.4 (PDF 14): "Increased volatility of market prices also contributed to greater haircuts, though participants in some markets said that it did not contribute materially." (H)
4. **Tri-party (S1, S2)**: in the tri-party segment, margins were set in custodial agreements, changed "only occasionally", and did not respond to CDS (S2 p.30). Some investors set uniform haircuts. S1 p.22 (PDF 24): "Other investors seek to only trade with dealers for which the probability of default is perceived to be zero. For these cases, the haircut is not relevant and so are simply set to a uniform level across asset classes." That is inconsistent with tri-party margins being continuously recalibrated to recent volatility. S1 p.22 does list as an expected driver: "haircuts differ across collateral types in relation to the volatility in the value of the collateral." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
5. **BoE FSR Apr 2007 (S11)**, contemporaneous counter-signals. p.38 (PDF 36): "most estimates of the direct exposures of prime brokers to hedge funds remain small relative to capital" and "estimates also suggest that the leverage of hedge funds has fallen since 2000". p.49 (PDF 47): "Margining and collateral practices, in particular, have been strengthened over this period [since LTCM 1998]." (CONTEMPORANEOUS, H)
6. **SSG p.14 (PDF 19)**, post-crisis remedy: "many firms are planning to change the volatility estimates in their VaR methodologies to make them more sensitive to volatility spikes. To this end, firms may use shorter horizon price histories or give greater weight to more recent observations". This makes measures more reactive to recent data, the opposite of a through-the-cycle fix. (RETROSPECTIVE, H)
7. **Tension inside FRBNY-sponsored sources**: S6 refers to "pro-cyclical increases in those margins" in tri-party, while S1/S2 find tri-party margins barely moved Jul 2008–Jan 2010. The S6 statements may refer to Aug 2007–Jun 2008 (not covered by S1/S2 data) or to other segments. This could not be resolved from the documents. (M)

## Q5. Competing mechanisms documented

### Sudden funding withdrawal (runs) vs margin spirals
- S2 p.29 (PDF 31): "when facing a crisis, a dealer should not expect to face higher margins when seeking funding in the tri-party repo market. Rather, cash investors are more likely to simply pull their funding." (H)
- S2 p.28 (PDF 30): Lehman's collateral "shrank by 12%" Sep 9–10 and fell by "26% from September 11 to 12". The Valukas Report is cited as describing much of the decline as "self-imposed", with additional declines that "may have been unplanned". (H)
- S1 p.37 (PDF 39), Bear Stearns, quoting Friedman 2010: "repo market lenders declined to roll over or renew repo loans, even when the loans were supported by high-quality collateral such as agency securities". (H. A secondary quote within S1. The Bear episode is outside the S1/S2 data window.)
- S1 p.45 (PDF 47): "We find little evidence of a market-wide run in the tri-party repo market." S2 p.40 (PDF 42): "there was no system-wide run on repo based on evidence from [...] the tri-party repo market." (H)
- S6 §6, 21 of 43 (PDF 46): "behavior started to trend closer to the behavior of unsecured credit investors, resulting in Cash Investors exiting the repo market or drastically changing their collateral requirements. Given the heavy reliance on the repo market for financing, this pull back in funding and the meaningful increases in margin requirements in a deteriorating market contributed to systemic risk concerns." (H. Cites both withdrawal and margin increases.)
- S7 p.vii (PDF 7): "In bad times, tightening is often implemented first through revisions to counterparty credit limits, while increases in haircuts tend to follow later." And: "The withdrawal of real money investors from securities lending programmes and associated repo investments, particularly following the Lehman bankruptcy, led to a severe contraction in the supply of secured financing." (H)
- Bilateral margin spirals: S12 (ACADEMIC) p.4 (PDF 6): the haircut index "rises from zero in early 2007 to nearly 50 percent at the peak of the crisis in late 2008". S2 and S1 also document large bilateral increases in segment 5 (see Q2). (RETROSPECTIVE)

### Leverage/exposure growth and deleveraging
- S1 p.46 (PDF 48) and S2 p.13 (PDF 15) attribute the post-Lehman fall in tri-party volume to "a general de-leveraging by dealers", maturing assets, valuation declines and the LSAP. S1 p.48 (PDF 50): "the decline in collateral appears to be more of a gradual de-leveraging (for reasons noted previously) rather than a sudden run." (H. The authors' interpretation, partly "anecdotal", S1 p.2.)
- Primary dealer financing growth 2001–2007 (Q4c table). (CONTEMPORANEOUS data, H)
- S7 p.vii (PDF 7): "the easing of credit terms – including the erosion of haircuts – contributed to the growth in leverage." (RETROSPECTIVE, H)

### Asset-quality / collateral deterioration
- S1 p.1 (PDF 3): less-liquid collateral share "was growing before the crisis" and "approached 30 percent" at peak. (H. No dated series given.)
- S1 p.46 (PDF 48): "The amount of Non Fed-eligible collateral more than halved between July 2008 and July 2009, falling from $600 billion to less than $300 billion". S2 p.16 (PDF 18): ABS collateral "declined by 66% (from about $75 billion to about $25 billion)" Jul 2008–Apr 2009, and corporates by "about $80 billion, or roughly 44%". (H)
- S1 p.58 (PDF 60): Lehman's non-Fed-eligible share rose "to 87% from about a quarter of Lehman's tri-party collateral". (H)
- S7 p.4 (PDF 14): "The key drivers of the increase in haircuts or even the ineligibility of some assets as collateral were market illiquidity, valuation uncertainty and counterparty credit concerns." (H)
- S6 §6, 21 of 43 (PDF 46): "Margin Levels: Margin levels in certain asset classes were insufficient to cover the close-out/liquidation risk of the securities held as collateral. Valuations: Market participants did not sufficiently anticipate the potential for some types of repo collateral to lose price transparency and liquidity for extended periods of time." (H)
- S9 p.5 (PDF 10): Aaa-corporate volatility applied to super-senior CDO tranches (quoted in 4a.9). (H)
- S12 p.16 (PDF 18), ACADEMIC: for BBB+/A corporates "in the first half of 2007 [...] the haircut was zero". It also says "The market disappeared for unpriced CLO/CDO, unpriced ABS/MBS/all subprime, and for AA-AAA CDOs." Footnote 18: "Repo rates and haircuts could be different for non-dealer bank counterparties, such as hedge funds." (RETROSPECTIVE, academic)

### Counterparty identity vs collateral
- S1 p.25 (PDF 27), quoting Valukas 2010: "in triparty repos, typically investors look to the counterparty (i.e., broker dealer) first and the collateral second when setting haircuts. In other words, a haircut may not be sufficient for an investor if it has serious concerns about the viability of its counterparty". (H, secondary quote)

### Stabilising policy (confounder)
- S1 p.68 (PDF 70): "Perhaps the biggest reason for the apparent stability of the market is the exceptional policy actions that were taken throughout the crisis, including the creation of the PDCF." S2 p.38–39 (PDF 40–41) discusses the PDCF (authorized March 16, 2008, closed February 1, 2010). (H. The whole S1/S2 sample lies within the PDCF period.)

## What could not be found
- No primary or official dated series of repo haircuts/margins (tri-party or bilateral) for any year before 2007.
- No official tri-party margin data for Aug 2007–Jun 2008 (Bear Stearns, Countrywide period). S1 fn.14 confirms the gap.
- No quantitative support was found for the "share of less liquid collateral was growing before the crisis" claim (S1). No dated pre-2007 tri-party composition series was located.
- The IMF GFSR October 2008 table mentioned in the brief was not located. The IMF table found is in the **April 2008** GFSR (Table 1.2). The IMF October 2008 URL tried returned 404.
- The published JFE (2012) version of Gorton–Metrick was not fetched. The single-dealer characterization comes from S2 ("a high-quality dealer"). The 2009 WP itself says "Our data comes from dealer banks".
- FSA hedge-fund counterparty survey contents and coverage were not obtained.
- Capital/liquidity buffer series (regulatory capital ratios, broker-dealer net capital, Z.1) were not verified in this audit.
