# Prior art and positioning

The project’s strongest contribution is a test for **when an organization has lost the ability to recover while recovery remains physically possible**. At the same state and deadline, ask whether the current rule can succeed, whether another use of existing capabilities can succeed, whether a missing capability can still be acquired in time, and whether recovery would be possible if the organizational restrictions were lifted. Following the same trajectory gives a last viable start for each class. The interval between two such dates measures the time lost to a specified constraint. [Argument I, §§I.3.5–I.3.7](Argument_I_Paradox_of_Adaptive_Organization.md)

The settlement shows why this distinction matters. A full store can hide the consequence of a decision: continuing to forage and starting to retrain can be equally good for near-term food stock, yet only one choice preserves a way to survive. The climate experiment isolates a different cost of delay by holding spending fixed and changing whether deployment capacity must first be built. Both examples support a diagnosis of lock-in by showing a feasible recovery under the relaxed assumptions and ruling out every recovery under the tighter ones. [Settlement experiment](Argument_I_Paradox_of_Adaptive_Organization.md) · [Climate experiment](Climate_Experiment.md)

**Evidence to report.** At tick 11 of the prescribed base comparison, the specialist holds 11 units of food and has lost its recovery option. The generalist holds nine and can still retrain. Across the 6,000 chosen settings, a distinct experiment finds that the forage-first decision rule enters lock-in in 4,940 settings, while the training-first rule survives in all 6,000. The figure of 1,581 measures an earlier deadline *together with* stock dominance for the prescribed pair. The model assigns the retraining costs. An empirical claim about how often firms encounter this mechanism would need independent measurements of costs, storage or slack, and actual decisions. [Argument I, §I.3.10 and Experiment E](Argument_I_Paradox_of_Adaptive_Organization.md)

Hannan, Pólos and Carroll offer an especially relevant comparison for the retraining assumption. Their formal model relates organizational architecture to the time and opportunity cost of reorganization and to mortality risk. That work supplies a possible explanation for differences in transition time. The settlement takes such a difference as an input and computes whether it closes a recovery window. [Hannan, Pólos & Carroll (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.14.5.463.16763)

**Evidence to report.** In the central 2°C example, a 30% annual capacity-growth ceiling permits a last start in 2040. Removing the capacity constraint while retaining the spending ceiling permits a last start in 2045. The five-year difference measures the effect of having to build capacity under those assumptions. A date in another paper can answer a different question about emissions reductions, renewable share, probability of meeting a target, or available spending. Numerical agreement across those definitions would not independently validate a closing year. [Climate experiment, §§2–3](Climate_Experiment.md)

The stopping-rule experiment isolates a second effect. Raising the permitted rate of capacity growth preserves every previously available schedule. Committing to keep ordering capacity can still shorten the recovery window because those orders compete with deploying abatement. Across three tested capacity costs, the program that keeps ordering has to start two to six years earlier than the program that stops ordering once capacity ceases to limit deployment. [Argument II, §II.6, Experiment E-II.2](Argument_II_Necessity_of_Acceleration.md)

**Terminology.** Unruh’s carbon lock-in concerns coevolving technologies and institutions sustained by increasing returns. Dow et al. frame adaptation limits around actors’ objectives and risks. In the IPCC glossary, a soft limit allows for options that may become available later. When using *lock-in* here, specify the horizon, target and interventions being relaxed. The reader can then tell exactly which recovery option has been lost. [Unruh (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0301-4215(00)00070-7) · [Dow et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate1847) · [IPCC AR6 glossary, “Adaptation limits”](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_SYR_Annex-I.pdf)

## What the synthesis adds to existing models

Strnad et al. used reinforcement learning to find sequences of governance decisions that kept a modeled economy within a planetary (climate) boundary and a social-foundation boundary. Renewable-energy knowledge grew through deployment. In Figure 5 the agent subsidizes renewables for most of the run. A carbon tax cannot be introduced immediately without breaching the social-foundation boundary, and is switched on only later. Their action menu was specified in advance. The project here also asks when changing the organization can still make a different action available before the deadline. [Strnad et al. (2019), Figure 5 and accompanying text](https://arxiv.org/html/1908.05567)

Mathias, Anderies and Janssen computed finite-horizon climate viability kernels, varied the maximum rate of change in emissions control, and traced the loss of feasible policies as departure from business as usual was delayed. Botta, Jansson and Ionescu formalized finite-horizon reachability, viability and avoidability for sequential decisions, then applied their verified policy framework to emissions decisions with uncertain implementation and technology availability. A later paper studied the repeated choice to start or delay a green transition. Botta and colleagues use viability to mean that a process can continue for a given number of steps. This project requires a continuation that also satisfies stated safety and terminal conditions. [Mathias et al. (2017), Methods and Figure 2](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep42061) · [Botta et al. (2017), §§3–4](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0956796817000156) · [2018 emissions application, §1.1](https://doi.org/10.5194/esd-9-525-2018) · [2023 start-or-delay application, §§3–4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10666-022-09867-w)

The computational contribution concerns the evidence needed to diagnose an individual case. Exhibiting one successful schedule settles whether recovery is possible. Concluding that the organization has lost every route to recovery also requires an exclusion that covers its admissible schedules. The settlement permits exhaustive computation. The climate model supplies a bound. For the general case, the machine-checked reduction explains how the search can inherit the difficulty of bounded planning. The planning lower bound and the graph characterization of restoration are established results used in making that argument. [Argument III, §§III.4–III.7](Argument_III_Adaptive_Escape_Problem.md) · [Bylander](https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702(94)90081-7) · [Feige & Kogan](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dam.2021.09.003)

## Suggested positioning for an introduction

How long can a target remain physically achievable after an organization has lost the ability to reach it? We compare the last viable start for each of four classes of intervention along the same trajectory. In a settlement model, a full store makes continued foraging and retraining equally attractive on a short-term food objective. How the settlement resolves that tie determines whether it retains a path to survival. In a climate model, we hold spending fixed and compute how much earlier mitigation must start when deployment capacity takes time and money to build. In both cases, diagnosing lock-in requires exhibiting a feasible recovery with the constraint relaxed and ruling out recovery with it in force. [Argument I](Argument_I_Paradox_of_Adaptive_Organization.md) · [Climate experiment](Climate_Experiment.md) · [Argument III](Argument_III_Adaptive_Escape_Problem.md)

**Scope of the review.** The sources cover viability and reachability, organizational capabilities, mitigation timing and deployment, and planning and target-set selection. The settlement and climate results establish mechanisms within specified models. Measuring the corresponding recovery windows in an actual organization would require independent observations of feasible actions, implementation costs and lead times.
